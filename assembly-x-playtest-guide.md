# Assembly X playtest guide

## Purpose

This first round tests whether learners can infer the meanings of grouping and duplication from the interaction, distinguish evidence from proof, and explain equivalence structurally. It is not a test of the learner.

## Recommended participants

- Three to five participants
- Ideally two or three learners near Grade 6 and one or two older beginners
- A mix of confidence levels in mathematics
- For minors, obtain the appropriate parent or guardian permission and avoid recording identifying information

## Session length

Allow 20–25 minutes:

- 2 minutes: welcome and setup
- 10–15 minutes: uninterrupted prototype use
- 5 minutes: follow-up questions
- 3 minutes: observer notes immediately afterward

## Opening script

> We are testing this learning experience, not you. Some parts may be unclear or may not work as expected. Please say what you are noticing, expecting, and wondering as you go. I may ask what you are thinking, but I will try not to help you solve the puzzles.

Do not explain the trays, sealed crates, duplicators, distributive property, or equivalence before the participant begins.

## Observer stance

- Ask “What are you expecting to happen?” before the participant presses Run.
- Ask “What do you think happened?” after an unexpected result.
- If the participant is stuck, first ask what they have tried.
- Avoid confirming whether an idea is correct until the interface provides its response.
- Record exact learner language when possible.
- Distinguish an interface problem from a mathematical misconception.

## Observation sheet

| Checkpoint | What to observe | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First screen | Does the learner understand how to install modules without instruction? | |
| First execution | Does the learner predict the effect of order? | |
| Level 3 | What does the learner think the tray or group represents? | |
| Level 4 | Can the learner explain why the 3 is or is not duplicated? | |
| Level 5 | Does visual inventory support the claim of equivalence? | |
| Level 6 | Does the learner initially treat the matching case as proof? | |
| Final build | Can the learner construct \(3x+6\) without trial-and-error cycling? | |
| Final explanation | Does the learner justify the rule using inventory, tests, or memorized vocabulary? | |

Also record:

- First action and time to first action
- Every program revision
- Places where the learner rereads text
- Controls that are missed or misinterpreted
- Moments of surprise, confidence, frustration, or explanation
- Whether the learner scrolls past important feedback

## Follow-up questions

1. What did the sealed \(x\)-crate mean to you?
2. What did placing objects in one tray change?
3. How would you explain the difference between \(2(x+3)\) and \(2x+3\)?
4. How can two different factory plans always produce the same result?
5. Why was the test at \(x=4\) not enough in the counterexample level?
6. Which moment made the mathematics clearest?
7. Which moment felt slow, confusing, or unnecessary?
8. If you could change one thing about the factory, what would it be?

## Success indicators for the first round

Treat these as design signals, not grades:

- Most participants begin without facilitator instruction.
- Participants predict that changing operation order can change the output.
- By Level 4, participants describe grouping as determining what gets duplicated.
- By Level 6, participants understand why one matching input does not prove equivalence.
- In the final explanation, participants refer to the three \(x\)-crates and six units.
- Participants can identify at least one moment when the factory’s behavior clarified an idea.
